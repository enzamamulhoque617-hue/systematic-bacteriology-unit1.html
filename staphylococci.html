🧫 What are Staphylococci?

Staphylococci are Gram-positive cocci that usually occur in irregular clusters resembling a bunch of grapes.

Simple idea:
Staphylococci = Round bacteria + Grape-like clusters + Catalase positive

Important examples

  • Staphylococcus aureus — important pathogen
  • Staphylococcus epidermidis — associated with medical devices
  • Staphylococcus saprophyticus — important cause of UTI
🧠 Memory Trick:
STAPH = “Stacks/Clusters + Catalase +”

🧫 Cultural Characteristics

Staphylococci are relatively easy to grow compared with many fastidious bacteria.

On ordinary media

  • Grow readily.
  • Colonies are usually round.
  • Colonies are smooth and raised.
  • Colonies are opaque.

On Blood Agar

  • Colonies may show haemolysis.
  • S. aureus commonly shows beta-haemolysis.
  • S. aureus may produce golden-yellow pigment.

On Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA)

MSA contains high salt concentration, which selects for salt-tolerant organisms such as Staphylococci.

S. aureus commonly ferments mannitol and produces acid, turning the medium yellow.

🧠 MSA = Salt + Mannitol

Salt → selects Staphylococci
Mannitol fermentation by S. aureus → Yellow

⚗️ Biochemical Reactions

Test Typical result Importance
Catalase Positive Separates Staphylococci from Streptococci
Oxidase Usually negative Useful identification test
Coagulase Species dependent S. aureus is usually positive
DNase Usually positive in S. aureus Supports identification of S. aureus
Mannitol fermentation Variable S. aureus usually positive
Glucose fermentation Generally positive Usually without gas

⭐ Catalase Test

Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide.

H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂

Oxygen produces visible bubbles.

Staphylococci = Catalase Positive → Bubbles

⭐ Coagulase Test

Coagulase causes plasma to clot.

S. aureus → usually coagulase positive.

🧠 High-yield:
Catalase + → Staphylococcus
Coagulase + → strongly suggests S. aureus

🦠 Pathogenesis

Pathogenesis means how the organism produces disease.

Colonization → Entry through wound → Attachment → Multiplication → Inflammation → Disease

Step 1 — Colonization

Staphylococci may normally live on the skin and in the nose without causing disease.

Step 2 — Entry

They can enter through cuts, wounds, burns, surgical sites or damaged skin.

Step 3 — Multiplication

The bacteria multiply at the site of infection.

Step 4 — Tissue damage

Enzymes and toxins produced by some species can damage tissues and help the bacteria spread.

Step 5 — Inflammation

The immune system responds, producing inflammation and often pus formation.

S. aureus is especially important because it can cause pyogenic (pus-forming) infections and toxin-mediated diseases.

🩺 Diseases Caused by Staphylococci

1. Skin and soft tissue infections

  • Abscess
  • Furuncle (boil)
  • Carbuncle
  • Impetigo
  • Wound infection

2. Deep infections

  • Pneumonia
  • Osteomyelitis
  • Endocarditis
  • Bacteremia
  • Septicemia

3. Toxin-mediated diseases

  • Food poisoning
  • Toxic shock syndrome
  • Scalded skin syndrome

Important species

Species Important association
S. aureus Pyogenic infections + toxin-mediated diseases
S. epidermidis Medical devices / foreign-body infections
S. saprophyticus Urinary tract infection

🔬 Laboratory Diagnosis

Specimen → Gram stain → Culture → Biochemical tests → Identification → Antibiotic susceptibility

1. Specimen

Depending on infection:

  • Pus
  • Wound swab
  • Blood
  • Sputum
  • Other appropriate clinical specimens

2. Gram staining

Shows:

Gram-positive cocci arranged in clusters.

3. Culture

Culture on suitable media such as blood agar. Mannitol Salt Agar may be useful for selective isolation.

4. Catalase test

Positive → bubbles.

5. Coagulase test

A positive result supports identification of S. aureus.

6. Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Performed to determine antimicrobial sensitivity/resistance. Resistance such as MRSA is clinically important.
